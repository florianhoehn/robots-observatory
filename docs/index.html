Why we publish a number we don't report

A straight byte comparison says the two copies differ on … of sites. That number is almost entirely artefact: two error pages, or two cookie-consent pages, whose bytes differ only because each carries a one-off token.

Genuine identity-dependent policy holds up on … sites. rakuten.co.jp gives a declared crawler 490 bytes of rules and a browser a 51-byte stub; telegraph.co.uk gives 3,228 bytes against 914. A further … let one through and refused the other — different access, not different policy.

The large number stays on the page so the gap between it and the real one is visible rather than quietly dropped.

Honesty

What this cannot tell you

It can

  • Say what share names AI crawlers, and which ones
  • Say what share publishes a guide, an opt-out or a reservation
  • Say what share asks for money
  • Say whether the answer changes by who asks
  • Show how much of a top-1000 list is not a website

It cannot

  • Say whether any of it is enforced. Declared and enforced are different datasets; this is the first
  • Say whether signing your requests helps — nothing was fetched that could be refused
  • Say anything about change over time, yet
  • Speak for other countries: one vantage, one connection
  • Speak for the long tail outside a popularity list
  • Speak for ad technology, 14 of which this connection cannot resolve

Method

How the panel was built

Tranco list 467YX, 18 October 2026, top 1000 — screened by hand down to 463 websites. The other 537 were dropped mostly because they are not websites: CDN and asset hosts, cloud endpoints, ad servers, DNS infrastructure. 31 of the top 50 are not pages anyone reads. Tranco ranks domains by how often machines look them up, and one human page view triggers a dozen machine lookups.

Every decision is published per host, so you can disagree with a single row instead of auditing a rule. A further 17 were dropped because this connection cannot resolve them, which is a fact about the measuring machine and recorded as one.

No page was ever fetched — only files the web publishes for machines to read, so no request was one a site asked crawlers not to make. Five requests per site, 1.5 seconds apart. That claim is checkable from two readable source files rather than merely asserted.

No model sits in the measurement path. Fetch, parse and count are deterministic, because the output of this project is the difference between runs — and a model that answered slightly differently each week would look exactly like the web changing its mind.
