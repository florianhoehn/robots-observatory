The correction

A rate measured on the top 1,000 is not a rate for the web

We ran the same probe twice: once on 463 hand-screened sites from the Tranco top 1,000, then on 16,314 unscreened sites from the top 20,000. The figures moved a long way.

Does the answer change by who asks

Almost never — and the number that says otherwise is wrong

Each site's robots.txt was fetched twice: once by a crawler that says what it is, once by something shaped like a browser.

Why we publish a number we don't report

A straight byte comparison says the two copies differ on … of sites. That number is almost entirely artefact: two error pages, or two cookie-consent pages, whose bytes differ only because each carries a one-off token.

Genuine identity-dependent policy holds up on … sites. telegraph.co.uk gives a declared crawler 3,228 bytes of rules and a browser 914; depositphotos.com gives 3,118 against 1,613. It runs the other way too: messenger.com hands the browser 841 bytes and the declared crawler 643. A further … let one identity through and refused the other — different access, not different policy.

The large number stays on the page so the gap between it and the real one is visible rather than quietly dropped.

Honesty

What this cannot tell you

It can

  • Say what share names AI crawlers, and which ones
  • Say what share publishes a guide, an opt-out or a reservation
  • Say what share asks for money
  • Say whether the answer changes by who asks
  • Show how much of a popularity list is not a website

It cannot

  • Say whether any of it is enforced. Declared and enforced are different datasets; this is the first
  • Say whether signing your requests helps — nothing was fetched that could be refused
  • Say anything about change over time, yet
  • Speak for other countries: one vantage, one connection
  • Speak for the long tail outside a popularity list
  • Speak for ad technology, 418 domains of which this connection cannot resolve
  • Be read as a floor rather than a count where a host timed out, errored, or answered everything with one stock page

Method

How the panel was built

Tranco list 467YX, 18 October 2026, top 20,000. Nothing was screened by hand: every domain that resolves to a server is in, whether or not it is a website anyone reads. That is the opposite of the smaller panel above, and deliberately so — hand-screening is a judgement, and a judgement applied 20,000 times is a thumb on the scale.

3,686 domains were dropped before probing, each for a stated reason: 3,190 have no address record at all and serve nothing from any vantage, 78 more fail to resolve or resolve ambiguously, and 418 are unreachable from this particular connection, which filters ad and tracker domains at the DNS layer. That last group is a fact about the measuring machine rather than the web, so it is published per host instead of quietly absorbed. Every exclusion is one row you can disagree with.

This run is marked degraded, and the figures are reported anyway. 13.6% of hosts returned at least one transport error — mostly TLS failures and connect timeouts, which is what the long tail of a popularity list actually looks like from one home connection. A further 797 hosts (4.9%) hit the two-minute per-host budget, so their later paths are unmeasured rather than absent, and 78 hosts answer every unknown path with one stock body and are discounted rather than counted as publishers. Each of those is a reason a share here reads low, never high.

No page was ever fetched — only files the web publishes for machines to read, so no request was one a site asked crawlers not to make. Twelve requests per site, 1.5 seconds apart. Four of the twelve are controls — security.txt, ads.txt, gpc.json, humans.txt — so the AI-specific numbers can be read against a site's general habit of publishing machine-readable files at all. That claim is checkable from two readable source files rather than merely asserted.

No model sits in the measurement path. Fetch, parse and count are deterministic, because the output of this project is the difference between runs — and a model that answered slightly differently each week would look exactly like the web changing its mind.
